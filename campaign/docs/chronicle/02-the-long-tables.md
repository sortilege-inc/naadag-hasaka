title: The Long Tables
part: The second, third and fourth days

## The second day

People kept arriving all through the second day, more than a trickle and less than a flood. There
were no set meals, only food that never ran out, and clusters of people sitting down to it around
mealtimes. There were pickup games and dice and gossip. Children ran underfoot. Cousins who had not
spoken in years caught up, and those who were looking flirted with those who were looking back.

Datsu carried his plate around and talked about the food. There was a cook named Ninosh, well known
in her own right, who cared where her ingredients came from, and she kept coming back to ask him
about his. Each time he had something different on the plate, and each time it was the same
conversation from a new angle. She was also plainly interested in him. He did not notice. She was
vegetarian, which he learned when he recommended the meat, and he recovered by remembering the
mushrooms.

There is no alcohol at a gathering like this, but there are drinks, mostly brewed from herbs and
fungus, that most people take the way others take weak beer. They leave a glow at the edges of
things and loosen the tongue. Makatooka had had a few of them. When Ninosh came round again she told
Datsu, loudly enough for Ninosh to hear, exactly what Ninosh wanted from him.

Ninosh turned to her. "Was I that obvious?"

"Oh, baby girl," Makatooka said. "You should probably put it back in your pants for a minute."

Ninosh looked embarrassed. She turned back to Datsu. "Well," she said, "seek me out if you're ever
so inclined." Datsu decided she was well enough known that he could find her if the need arose.

A little later Makatooka ran into a colleague from the hospital, one she had never got on with. The
colleague was still working. Makatooka was not.

"Hey," Makatooka said. "How's work?"

"It's been better, actually."

"Well, really glad I'm not there."

"I guess I can see why. I guess I shouldn't expect you back any time soon. Or maybe never. I don't
know." The colleague walked off, and Makatooka ordered another. Datsu went and found her a plate of
ordinary mushrooms, the kind that are only food.

Wasawi was sixteen. She had been formally acknowledged as an adult not long before, and nobody her
age had caught up with that yet. The other young people were boring and did not know how to have a
good time. She took a bottle and went into the forest park in the middle of the city, where you can
lose sight of the buildings even though a highway is a mile and a half away. She looked for
mushrooms. She jumped off rocks. She stayed out all night. A few years earlier she would have been
scolded for it; now there would only be comments, and it was her right. Tomorrow there would be
structure and speeches, and she had had enough of people.

Sign had found, after meeting Grandmother, that she felt safe. She was where she was supposed to be,
so she could stop hunting and let herself enjoy the place. She talked to everyone, loudly and
awkwardly. She kept coming back to Datsu's table for more ("You got the real good snacks, you know
what I'm talking about"), and Datsu greeted her each time with *oh, you again.* The uncles' dice game
had been going for a day and a half, and they had adopted her as a luck charm. She never took a seat
at the table, but she threw for whichever of them asked. Once she threw two twelves.

For Suva the second day was the last day to get ready. Tomorrow she would have to be present, and
after that the ceremony was hers. She felt the pressure. She was also irritated that Wasawi was not
there to help, off somewhere when there was family work to be done. The medicines and preparations
had been made ahead of time. What was left was to set everything out, to ready the room where
Grandmother would sit, to cleanse it, and to meditate and pray herself into the right frame of mind.
The healer who would attend with her had already been through and set up their own things. Suva
worked through hers slowly, knowing it would all be disturbed in a few days, and found it quieted
her. She said her good-nights before she went in, and did not come back out to the guests. She went
to bed at peace.

## The third day

The third day started late. Small groups had their morning rituals; by mid-morning the tables had
been pushed together into a rough U, with Grandmother at the centre and her immediate family on
either side. It was loud, and every so often someone stood and it quieted. A person would repeat a
piece of wisdom Grandmother had given them at a turning point in their life, or tell a story about
trouble they got into as a child and how she called them on it. An honoured member of the Council of
Twelve came for part of the day. They spoke about what Grandmother had built in her neighbourhood,
work that had been noticed across the city and copied in other neighbourhoods.

Suva stood up. She said what it had meant to learn from Grandmother as a mother, and how much of the
way she raised her own children came from her. She told one story: when her daughter was about
Wasawi's age, Suva had not been able to let her grow up, and Grandmother had helped her through it.
You honour those feelings, she had said, but they do not get to decide what you do.

Brunch ran into lunch and into the afternoon. Around two or three there was a break, a rest, walks.
In the evening they came back together for the gift-giving and then the feast.

The feast was the reason Datsu's people had come, and the cooks had been using him as extra labour
all day while everyone else rested. There were smoked meats, and mushroom dishes as extravagant as
anything else on the table. He was happier watching people load their plates than eating.

Grandmother had been making gifts for a year: beadwork, embroidery, small beautiful things. Sign and
Datsu, and others who were part of the wider circle rather than the family, were given pieces of
these. Sign's was a necklace, and she could feel that it had something to do with the spirit world.

For Makatooka, a relative came over on Grandmother's behalf carrying a folded blanket. Makatooka
unfolded it. It had been a falcon. Someone had reworked it, carefully, into a fox. She saw the fox
first. Then she saw the falcon underneath it and understood what she was holding.

She tried not to cry and could not keep her composure. She stood there holding the blanket against
herself and wept.

Sign was sitting nearby. "Oh, honey," she said. "Come here." Makatooka fell into her arms and Sign
held on.

When she could, Makatooka folded the blanket and put it away safely in the cubbies where the guests
kept their things. Then she went and found a stronger drink. The evening had lost its joy for her.

Grandmother came to Suva herself, carrying something wrapped in cloth. It was a set of jewellery she
had worn only a handful of times, a family heirloom, the kind of thing handed down the way a wedding
ring is.

"I never had a daughter of my own," Grandmother said, "and my daughter-in-law is gone. You are the
closest thing to a daughter I still have. I want you to have these."

Suva had not known she mattered that much. "Thank you," she said. "Would it be all right if I wore
them here, for the ceremony?"

"I would be so honoured if you would."

Grandmother was shorter than she had been, and bent. When she embraced Suva it was the embrace of a
very old woman, and Suva felt how much it cost her.

## The fourth day

On the fourth day the family told Grandmother's life. Three or four storytellers passed it between
them, from her childhood on. The early part was thin, and there were gaps in it that were clearly
left there on purpose. The story only filled out with her late adolescence, her arrival in Cahokia,
and meeting her husband, who had died about fifteen years ago. His death had been quiet. A Seeing is
not common, and not everyone is given one.

Sign listened for the gaps. Whoever had reached her through the Black had known how to do it, and
someone living Grandmother's life in the city would not have spent it the way Sign spent hers. She
went into the omissions and came out certain of something: that the missing years had to do with
Grandmother and the Black. Grandmother had met something there and had been getting away from it
since. It was dangerous. The message Sign took from the silence was not *stay away*. It was that she
was better off not knowing there was anything to stay away from.

Then came the speeches, from community leaders and elders. Suva gave one. She spoke about
Grandmother's place in the family and in the community, about the children Grandmother had lost and
the other things she had come through, and how she had been something the family could lean on. When
Suva lost her own daughter, it was Grandmother who pulled her through.

At some point before the fifth day Grandmother met privately with them, and asked them to
carry a box back to the Diné Republic.
