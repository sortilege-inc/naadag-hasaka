---
title: The Road to Cahokia
part: The first day of the Seeing
---

A Seeing is not an ordinary death rite. It is for a respected elder who has chosen the day of her
own death, and it runs ten days. The first two are arrivals: guests come in when they can, greet
each other, eat. The third and fourth are the long tables, the speeches and the stories. On the
fifth and sixth the elder withdraws with the few who will attend her, and the guests grow quieter.
On the seventh she dies, privately, with only a handful of people in the room. The eighth and ninth
are for the body and for mourning. The tenth is for farewells.

Grandmother Naadag, who came to Cahokia from the Diné Republic as a young woman and was nearly a
hundred and twenty-one, had chosen hers.

## Suva

Suva had been part of the family for forty years. Her own people had died in a fire when she was in
her early twenties, and she had married into her husband's family, farmers, and made it hers. She
was sixty-two now, a healer on the Path of the Snake, and her work was death and trauma: the
ceremonies at the end of a life, and the spiritual side of medicine.

When Grandmother decided on a Seeing, she asked Suva to be one of the people with her at the end.
Suva said yes. She had assisted at these before. She had never led one. It felt like being taken
the rest of the way into the family, and it made her nervous, because others in the family cared a
great deal about how it was done and she did not want to let them down. There were younger healers
among her nieces, and she wanted to show them how it went.

On the first day she was a hostess by proxy. She checked on her grandchildren, Wasawi and her
little brother, who had lost their mother not long before. She checked on the cousins and the
uncles. She had the ceremony in her hands already, and she knew it.

## Datsu

Datsu was thirty-two, a scout on the Path of the Raccoon, and he did not like cities. His people
kept herds out west, near the long stretch of wild country everyone had agreed to leave to the
animals, and he had been out following the buffalo, counting them, out of signal for
days. When he came back into range there was a message waiting.

His people had supplied this family with meat for years. It had started as business and turned into
friendship; they knew what the family liked and the family knew it would get a fair price. So they
were going to Cahokia together, with gifts and enough food to feed a crowd for more than a week:
meat, and the things that grow wild and will not grow in a garden, mushrooms above all. Datsu went
with them.

## Sign

Sign was forty-two and came from the Diné Republic, a Seeker on the Path of the Beaver. She had
spent part of her life around criminal organisations, and the part she cared about now was justice.
When someone was hurt, it stayed with her.

She had been in Coyote City looking into a wrong done to a poor man. The more she dug the more
there was, until she was reaching into the Black for it. Then, in a night of bad sleep, she had a
vision. Someone was reaching back to her through the Black. She did not know who. She knew the name
of a family, and she felt something pouring off that family, and she felt where it came from: an old
woman at its centre. She dropped the investigation and went.

She did not have a map. She had a direction. When a ride ran out she walked.

That is how Datsu's people found her, walking down a bend in the road. Out there you stop for
anyone on foot, and they stopped. She asked where they were going.

"The Seeing for a family in Cahokia," Datsu said. "If you want a ride into town, we're going that
way anyway."

She asked the family's name. When he said Naadag she stopped hearing the rest of the conversation,
and then she was in his face telling him they were going to be the best of friends. She was going
with him the whole way. Datsu, who had deliveries and deadlines, did not trust that one bit.

## Cahokia

They reached the city the evening before the Seeing began. Datsu took her as far as the edge of the
grounds by the lake, where the family's people were housed, and told her this was where she could
hop off.

"May the winds guide every movement that you do," Sign said, and went into the city.

She had never been to Cahokia. At home she knew every street and whom to ask for a secret; here she
knew nothing, and she stood out. The Diné dress differently, and people stared, or came over to ask,
or kept away. She wandered and let the city tell her where to go. More than once she drifted into
places a stranger had no business being, and people let her drift out again, which was luck. Her wrist device was set up for the Diné Republic's network and would not
talk to Cahokia's, so she found a public terminal and looked up the Seeing: the place, and when to
arrive.

## The first day

On the first morning Sign sat down in the park across from the grounds and went into the Black. She
wanted to know whether this place felt like her vision.

She did not get an answer to that. She found herself in a pueblo, among people in old clothes, going
about their business. No one spoke to her. She walked for what felt like half an hour. Then she
turned a corner and saw the town from an angle she knew, and understood that it was Oraibi, the
oldest town in the Diné Republic, as it might have looked long before the Awis. She came out of it
not knowing if she was in the right place, knowing only that she had come a long way and seen
something she had not seen before, and she went up to the house.

Makatooka was already there, sitting in a corner, and had not come forward to say hello.

She was twenty-eight, a healer, a physician on leave from her hospital. Years ago she had been close
to this family. Zibizin, the partner of Suva's daughter, used to bring little Wasawi in to be
patched up; by the third time it was "you again", and after that the two of them were friends.
Zibizin taught her to fly. Then Zibizin went missing, seven years ago now, and it was the same year
Makatooka's own life came apart. She had not refused the family's calls since. She had not answered
them either. When the invitation came, she took it because she was trying to mend what she had done.

Suva saw her and went over slowly. Makatooka was a little younger than Suva's daughter had been when
she died. There was grief in that, and Suva was worried about where Makatooka had been and what she
had been doing, but she wanted her in. Makatooka let herself be mothered. There was a hug. She
wanted to apologise and decided it could wait until there were fewer people watching.

Then Sign came in, quiet and a little lost, and asked if she could talk to someone who knew what was
going on here. She had never met the woman at the centre of this, she said, but she had felt her
from a long way off.

"The family is usually very welcoming," Suva said. "Right now we're having our Seeing for
Grandmother Naadag."

The questions Sign asked told Suva two things: that this stranger had never met Grandmother, and
wanted to, and that she was Diné, like Grandmother. Suva took her over.

Grandmother was doing well for her age. She was mostly clear, and with help she could get up and go
around the room in her own small orbit, then rest. Most of the time she sat in a comfortable chair
and people came to her. She was more ceremonial than spiritual. She held that there was something
rooted in tradition worth keeping, even if not always in the same form, and, now that Suva thought
about it, the family's rites had always been heavier on form than on spirit.

She took Sign's hand and held it for the whole conversation.

"My name is Sign," Sign said. "I've come all the way from the Diné Republic. There was this feeling.
It came to me like a lance, from the spirit world. I haven't really understood why. But I know that
you're at the centre of it, and I need to understand it."

Grandmother looked closely into her eyes. "What is your path?"

"Beaver."

"I see you are a Beaver," Grandmother said. "I have a gift for you. But it will need to wait."

After that Sign sat at the edges and watched, and tried to wait properly.

There were about fifty people by then; there would be two hundred when it was full. Makatooka drifted
from group to group and did not stay anywhere long. Datsu had a tray of the food his people had
brought and was carrying it round, bragging about it. He offered her some fruit leather. In the
corner a table of uncles had had a dice game going all afternoon.
