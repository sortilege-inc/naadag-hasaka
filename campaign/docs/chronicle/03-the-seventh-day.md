## Tika

Tika was nineteen, thin and gangly, and he came to the Seeing as his sister's plus-one.

He and his older sister Yohipa had grown up in the Haudenosaunee Confederacy. Anyone can become a
citizen there, but a family that keeps its own language and ways instead of assimilating gets the
smaller share of what is going, and theirs had kept theirs. The two of them left, with no one but each
other, and came to Cahokia. Yohipa was about twenty-five now and ran a successful restaurant.
Grandmother had helped her get it started and find her feet in the neighbourhood. Her kitchen was
one of several feeding the Seeing.

Tika had a cybernetic eye, an orb that made no attempt to look like a real one, and a small drone that
followed him everywhere. He had done judo after school for years and knew the moves better than his
body could do them. He loved cooking. He loved games more, and he gambled; he had won and lost large
sums more than once, and at the moment he was broke. He worked in his sister's restaurant, and she
was trying to get him to find something else.

His real interest was cybernetics. He wanted to build something that would give a person what the
Adanadi gives, without the Adanadi.

On the first two days he tried to charm everyone. The eye started a lot of conversations. One of them
was with Choyan, someone in their early fifties who knew their way around the kind of work his eye
was an example of. He told Choyan his theories. Choyan was kind about it, and by the end of the
conversation had crossed several of them off: that had been tried, and that one was settled in the journals he
did not read.

The uncles did not make room for him at their table, so he got up a game of his own: dice, but with
bluffing, a game he thought was better than what people usually played. He won a favour. The person he
won it from had been talking up a job, and now it was Tika's: apprentice mechanic on a vehicle taking
people from the Seeing back west, keeping it running, a third of the pay up front and two thirds at
the end. It was not glamorous. It was more interesting than cooking the same meals all day.

## The fifth and sixth days

The fifth and sixth days were quiet. The kitchens closed; the food was set out ready to eat. Some
guests slept for two days straight. Some meditated, or walked the paths along the lake and the river.
Datsu took himself off to the forest paths, away from people, and started going through what the
journey would need.

Makatooka went looking for kalera root and could not find any. She drank a strong tea instead, twice
as strong as she usually took it. She had long since learned to keep her use under a certain limit,
and this time she went past it. What she saw frightened her. She tried to keep it to herself and did
not manage it. The others resting in the same hall noticed; one gave her a sideways look, and another
made a show of noticing nothing at all.

For Suva the fifth and sixth days were the work. She and the other attending healer had two days to
bring Grandmother, body, mind and spirit, to the point where she was at peace with what would happen
at dawn on the seventh. They bathed her and massaged her. They burned sage. They braided her hair.
They meditated with her and fanned her, and chanted, and one or the other of them was nearly always
singing, a little hum more often than a song, and sometimes Grandmother joined in.

Suva had been nervous about leading it. What she found was that the preparation had done its job:
each thing led into the next, and she could be there with Grandmother instead of worrying about the
next step. Once, at the end of a bath, when Suva had her under the arms to help her up, Grandmother
began to sob and leaned into her, and Suva held her until it was done.

On the sixth day Suva noticed that one of the closet doors was ajar. It struck her as odd. Her partner
was sleeping, and she meant to ask about it later.

They kept watch through the night in shifts. At about half past four, before dawn, Suva heard a door
in the next room. Nobody should have been there. The room looked as they had left it.
She went into the Black, the way she can, with her senses set aside and a self that can move, and
felt someone close by, in the closet. She moved through the door. Wasawi was sitting inside, crammed
in, doing muscle-relaxation exercises to keep herself awake.

In a couple of hours the room would be filled with the compound that would stop Grandmother's heart.
Wasawi had not taken what the attendants take against it.

Suva was furious. She did not want to make it public: it would dishonour the family, and Grandmother.
She could not leave the room without breaking what they had spent two days making clean. Wasawi was
already in it. So Suva pulled her out of the closet, gave her the blocker, and told her in a very few
words how close she had come to ruining everything. She was to go back in, and if Suva heard one
sound from her, or anyone else found her, she would be in the worst trouble of her life. Wasawi had
nearly fallen into the room when the door opened. She was exhausted, and she had just understood how
close she had come to dying. She did what she was told.

Around the same hour Tika went out to a public place and did martial-arts forms, as showily as he
could. After a while a few people came over to watch, and one of them offered to spar. It did not last
long. He tried again, and it ended the same way; they were faster and saw openings he could not. They
ended up sparring each other and left him to it.

Suva spent the last hours before dawn wearing Grandmother's jewellery, thinking back over their life
together and how much she owed her.

## The seventh day

They woke Grandmother, and she went out and saw the dawn. Then she came into the room, and they lit
the compound. The smoke was thick. The two healers had taken the blocker; to them it was only smoke.

They sat on low cushions facing her and held her hands, the three of them, holding her upright between
them. Suva told her what she had meant to them, and wished her well in the crossing, and promised they
would be with her the whole way. Grandmother began to sweat. She sang, and they sang with her. Suva
found herself singing a song she knew but could not place. She knew it from her own childhood.

The smoke took her. Her body went slack. Suva felt the pulse at her wrist and then felt nothing. They
laid her back, and after a little while they dressed her one last time. Then they slept for the rest
of the day.

## The eighth day

On the eighth day the family brought the body out, and people came to say goodbye. At the busiest
times there was a short line. Some spoke, some sang, some stood in silence. Wasawi went up and told
her she was sorry. Makatooka went up with the blanket around her shoulders and softly said something
like a poem. Datsu waited for a quiet moment and went up to thank her for asking his people to bring
the food.

She was buried beside her husband. The burial was short, and most of the guests were there for it.
Some left as soon as it was over.

## Packing up

Datsu's people started packing straight away. The vehicle going west was not theirs. It was a
hover barge, about the size of a bus and three times as wide, and it needed a crew of three to fly and
maintain it with anything spare; they were one short, and the replacement had already been found.

"I'm Tika," Tika told him. "The new mechanic."

"Excellent. I heard we were having a change of personnel. Welcome to the group."

Datsu looked at the eye and hoped it meant the kid was good with machines. Tika was a decent pilot
in the city, mostly of drones, and he had never been out anywhere really rural. He did not tell his
sister how he had got the job. She owed him money for his work at the restaurant and could not pay
it before he left; she said she would have it when he got back.

Suva had very little time. She told her husband about the journey Grandmother had sent them on, and
that she was taking their granddaughter with her because Wasawi needed guidance, and that he would
have their grandson to himself for a while. The family would help. Then she told Wasawi.

Toward the end of the eighth day a cousin came to say that money had been made available for the
trip, and credit at some of the local shops, a good deal of it. Suva handed food and shelter to Datsu,
who had done more travelling, and saw to the medicines and the spiritual comforts herself. Makatooka
packed her trauma kit and loaded what she knew about medicinal herbs into the device on her wrist. She left the blanket somewhere safe. She brought her cat, Wings.

The guests went home on the ninth and tenth days, and the travellers meant to leave on the morning
of the eleventh, when the last of them had gone.

## The tenth day

Late on the morning of the tenth, it occurred to Suva that no one had seen Sign.

Wasawi came back looking worried. Sign was in her room, and she would not wake up. Every so often she
shook.

The healers who looked at her said that she was not in any danger they could see. Everything about
her body was as it should be, except the shaking, and that she would not wake. They decided to take
her with them and look after her on the way, until she did.
